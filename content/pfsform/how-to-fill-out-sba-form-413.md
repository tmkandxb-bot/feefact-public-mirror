# How to Fill Out SBA Form 413: Line-by-Line Instructions

SBA Form 413 is the Personal Financial Statement that the U.S. Small Business Administration uses to get a picture of what you personally own and what you personally owe. This guide walks through the current version of the form (version 05-24, OMB No. 3245-0188, expires 08/31/2027) from the first box to the signature line. For each part you will find three things: what the form asks, what counts, and common mistakes to avoid.

This guide is written for someone who has never seen the form. It explains how to complete it. It does not tell you whether you will be approved for anything, and nothing here is legal, tax, or accounting advice. Where a detail depends on the official instructions printed with the form, or on your lender, this guide says so instead of guessing.

## Before you start

### Understand how the form is built

Form 413 has a header, a summary, and a set of detail schedules:

- **Header** — who you are, which business the statement supports, and the date the numbers are "as of."
- **Section 1** — a one-page summary: your assets, your liabilities, your net worth, your annual income sources, and your contingent liabilities.
- **Sections 2 through 8** — the detail behind the summary: notes payable, stocks and bonds, real estate, other property and assets, unpaid taxes, other liabilities, and life insurance.
- **Signature and date** — at the end.

The single most useful thing to know is that Section 1 is a summary of the later sections. Several lines in Section 1 are totals of items you list one by one further down. If a total in Section 1 does not agree with its schedule, the statement contradicts itself.

For that reason, many people find it easier to complete the detail sections first and carry the totals back to Section 1. This guide follows the order of the form, but you can work in whichever order keeps your numbers consistent.

### Pick one date and stick to it

The header asks for a statement date. Every balance and every value on the form should be the figure as of that date. Decide on the date first, then gather the records that show your balances on or near it.

### Gather your records

The form asks for numbers you probably do not know from memory. Having these in front of you saves time:

- Statements for checking, savings, and retirement accounts
- Brokerage statements for stocks and bonds
- Loan statements for every note, auto loan, other installment loan, and mortgage, showing the current balance and the payment
- Purchase records for any real estate you own
- Life insurance policy statements showing cash surrender value and any policy loans
- Any notices or records of taxes you owe but have not paid
- Records of money that other people owe you

## Header

### What it asks

The header collects:

- Your name and address (you are the applicant)
- The business name
- The statement date
- The loan purpose, chosen from the programs listed on the form: 7(a) / 504 / surety, 8(a), WOSB, or disaster loan
- The business type
- Your marital status
- Your Social Security number

### What counts

- **Name and address:** your own, as an individual. This is a personal statement, so it describes you, not the company.
- **Business name:** the business that the loan or program application is for.
- **Statement date:** the "as of" date for every figure on the form.
- **Loan purpose:** the program you are applying under. If you do not know which one applies, ask your lender or the office handling your application rather than guessing.
- **Business type:** how the business is organized. Use the choices printed on the form. [VERIFY: exact business-type options on version 05-24]
- **Marital status:** your current status as of the statement date. Use the choices printed on the form. [VERIFY: exact marital-status options on version 05-24]
- **SSN:** your own Social Security number.

### Common mistakes

- Putting the business's address or tax ID where your personal details belong.
- Leaving the statement date blank, or using a date that does not match the balances you report.
- Marking the wrong program. [VERIFY: whether version 05-24 allows more than one loan purpose to be marked]
- Skipping marital status because it seems unrelated to a business loan. It is part of the header; fill it in.

## Section 1: The balance sheet summary

Section 1 is the heart of the form. It has four parts: assets, liabilities (ending in net worth), annual income sources, and contingent liabilities.

### Assets

#### What it asks

Section 1 lists these asset lines:

1. Cash
2. Savings
3. IRA / retirement accounts
4. Accounts and notes receivable
5. Life insurance — cash surrender value
6. Stocks and bonds
7. Real estate
8. Automobiles
9. Other personal property
10. Other assets

Add them together for total assets.

#### What counts

- **Cash:** money you can reach right away, as of the statement date.
- **Savings:** balances in savings accounts as of the statement date.
- **IRA / retirement:** balances in retirement accounts as of the statement date.
- **Accounts and notes receivable:** money other people owe you personally.
- **Life insurance — cash surrender value:** the cash surrender value of your policies, not the death benefit. The policies themselves are described in Section 8.
- **Stocks and bonds:** the total of the holdings you itemize in Section 3.
- **Real estate:** the total market value of the properties you itemize in Section 4.
- **Automobiles:** the value of vehicles you own.
- **Other personal property:** belongings of value that do not fit the lines above. You describe them in Section 5.
- **Other assets:** anything you own that has not been captured elsewhere. You describe these in Section 5 as well.

#### Common mistakes

- Entering the face amount (death benefit) of a life insurance policy instead of its cash surrender value.
- Writing a stocks-and-bonds or real estate figure in Section 1 that does not equal the total in Section 3 or Section 4.
- Counting the same asset on two lines — for example, a retirement account that holds stocks listed once under IRA / retirement and again under stocks and bonds.
- Listing a value for other personal property or other assets and then leaving Section 5 empty.
- Mixing in assets that belong to the business. This statement is about your personal finances.

### Liabilities and net worth

#### What it asks

Section 1 lists these liability lines:

1. Accounts payable
2. Notes payable
3. Installment account — auto, with the monthly payment
4. Installment account — other, with the monthly payment
5. Loans against life insurance
6. Mortgages on real estate
7. Unpaid taxes
8. Other liabilities

Add them together for total liabilities. Then calculate net worth:

**Net worth = total assets − total liabilities**

#### What counts

- **Accounts payable:** bills you owe that are not covered by another line.
- **Notes payable:** the total current balance of the notes you itemize in Section 2.
- **Installment account (auto):** what you still owe on vehicle loans, plus the monthly payment in the space provided.
- **Installment account (other):** what you still owe on other installment debts, plus the monthly payment.
- **Loans against life insurance:** amounts borrowed against your policies. These tie to the policies described in Section 8.
- **Mortgages on real estate:** the total of the mortgage balances you itemize in Section 4.
- **Unpaid taxes:** taxes you owe and have not paid. You describe them in Section 6.
- **Other liabilities:** debts that do not fit any line above. You describe them in Section 7.

#### Common mistakes

- Entering a monthly payment where the balance belongs, or the reverse. The installment lines ask for both: the amount owed and the monthly payment.
- Leaving out the monthly payment on the installment lines.
- Reporting the original loan amount instead of what you owe on the statement date.
- A mortgage total that does not match Section 4, or a notes payable total that does not match Section 2.
- Arithmetic errors. Total assets minus total liabilities must equal the net worth you write down. Check the subtraction twice. Net worth can be a small number or a negative number; report what the math produces.
- Listing an asset without its matching debt — for example, a car under automobiles with no auto installment balance when a loan is still outstanding.

### Annual income sources

#### What it asks

Section 1 asks for your sources of annual income.

#### What counts

Report yearly figures, not monthly ones, on the lines the form provides. [VERIFY: the exact income line labels on version 05-24]

#### Common mistakes

- Entering a monthly amount on an annual line.
- Leaving a source blank when it applies to you.
- Reporting the business's revenue as your personal income.

### Contingent liabilities

#### What it asks

Section 1 also asks about contingent liabilities.

#### What counts

A contingent liability is a debt you do not owe today but could become responsible for if something happens — the classic example is a loan you co-signed or guaranteed for someone else. Use the lines printed on the form to decide what belongs here. [VERIFY: the exact contingent-liability line labels on version 05-24]

#### Common mistakes

- Skipping this part because nothing is currently due. The point of the question is debts that are *not yet* due from you.
- Assuming you know how contingent amounts relate to total liabilities. [VERIFY: whether contingent liabilities are excluded from total liabilities on version 05-24]

## Section 2: Notes payable detail

### What it asks

For each note, Section 2 asks for:

- The holder of the note (who you owe)
- The original balance
- The current balance
- The payment amount
- The payment frequency
- The collateral, if the note is secured

### What counts

List each note on its own row. The current balances should add up to the notes payable line in Section 1.

### Common mistakes

- Combining several loans into one row.
- Leaving the frequency blank, so a reader cannot tell whether a payment is monthly, quarterly, or annual.
- Leaving the collateral column blank without saying whether the note is secured or unsecured.
- A Section 2 total that does not match Section 1.

## Section 3: Stocks and bonds detail

### What it asks

Section 3 is the itemized list behind the stocks and bonds line in Section 1.

### What counts

List each holding on its own row, using the columns printed on the form. [VERIFY: exact column headings in Section 3 on version 05-24] Use values as of your statement date.

### Common mistakes

- Writing one lump sum instead of itemizing holdings.
- Using values from a statement dated well before or after the statement date.
- A Section 3 total that does not match the stocks and bonds line in Section 1.

## Section 4: Real estate detail

### What it asks

For each property, Section 4 asks for:

- The type of property
- The address
- The purchase date
- The original cost
- The present market value
- The mortgage holder
- The mortgage balance
- The mortgage payment
- The status of the mortgage

### What counts

Give each property its own column or block. The market values should add up to the real estate line under assets in Section 1, and the mortgage balances should add up to the mortgages line under liabilities.

### Common mistakes

- Confusing original cost with market value. Cost is what you paid; market value is what the property is worth as of the statement date.
- Listing a property but leaving out its mortgage, or listing a mortgage with no matching property.
- Leaving the mortgage status blank.
- Section 4 totals that do not agree with Section 1 on either the asset side or the liability side.

## Sections 5 through 8: Descriptions

These four sections are written descriptions. Each one explains a number you entered in Section 1.

### Section 5: Other personal property and other assets

**What it asks:** a description of what makes up the other personal property and other assets lines.

**What counts:** name each item and its value clearly enough that a reader can see how you reached the Section 1 figures.

**Common mistakes:** vague entries such as "misc." and descriptions whose values do not add up to the Section 1 amounts.

### Section 6: Unpaid taxes

**What it asks:** a description of the unpaid taxes you reported in Section 1.

**What counts:** describe what is owed, using the prompts printed on the form. [VERIFY: the specific details Section 6 requests on version 05-24]

**Common mistakes:** entering an amount in Section 1 and leaving Section 6 empty, or the reverse.

### Section 7: Other liabilities

**What it asks:** a description of the other liabilities you reported in Section 1.

**What counts:** describe each debt and its amount.

**Common mistakes:** repeating debts that already appear in Section 2 or Section 4, which counts them twice.

### Section 8: Life insurance held

**What it asks:** a description of the life insurance you hold.

**What counts:** describe your policies using the prompts printed on the form. [VERIFY: the specific details Section 8 requests on version 05-24] What you write here should be consistent with the cash surrender value under assets and any life insurance loans under liabilities.

**Common mistakes:** describing a policy here but reporting no cash surrender value in Section 1 without explanation, or reporting a policy loan in Section 1 with no policy described here.

## Signature and date

### What it asks

The form ends with a signature and a date.

### What counts

Read the whole form, including anything printed near the signature line, before you sign. [VERIFY: what text appears with the signature block on version 05-24] Sign your own name and enter the date you actually sign.

### Common mistakes

- Submitting the form unsigned.
- Signing but leaving the date blank.
- Changing numbers after signing and reusing the old signature and date.

## A final check before you hand it in

1. Every header field is complete, including the statement date and loan purpose.
2. Total assets minus total liabilities equals the net worth shown.
3. Notes payable in Section 1 equals the total of Section 2.
4. Stocks and bonds in Section 1 equals the total of Section 3.
5. Real estate and mortgages in Section 1 equal the totals of Section 4.
6. Every "other" amount in Section 1 is explained in Sections 5 through 8.
7. Both installment lines show a balance and a monthly payment.
8. The form is signed and dated.

Completing Form 413 carefully does not guarantee any decision on your application. What it does is remove the avoidable problems — missing fields, mismatched totals, and unsigned pages — so the people reading your statement can focus on the numbers themselves.
