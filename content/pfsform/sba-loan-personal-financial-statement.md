# The SBA Loan Personal Financial Statement: Why Lenders Ask for It and How to Get It Right

If you are applying for an SBA-backed loan for the first time, one of the documents you will be asked for is a personal financial statement. For SBA purposes that document is SBA Form 413 (current version 05-24, OMB No. 3245-0188, expires 08/31/2027). It surprises many first-time borrowers: the loan is for the business, so why does the lender want to know about your savings account, your house, and your car loan?

This guide explains why the statement is required, who has to complete one, what lenders look at when they read it, and how to check your own form before you submit it. It does not predict or promise any outcome for your application, and it is not legal, tax, or accounting advice.

## What the personal financial statement is

Form 413 is a snapshot of your personal finances on one date. It lists what you own (assets), what you owe (liabilities), and the difference between the two (net worth). It also asks for your annual income sources and your contingent liabilities, and then asks you to back up the summary with detail schedules.

The form is used across several SBA programs. The header asks you to mark the purpose: 7(a) / 504 / surety, 8(a), WOSB, or disaster loan.

It is organized like this:

- **Header:** applicant name and address, business name, statement date, loan purpose, business type, marital status, and Social Security number.
- **Section 1:** the summary — assets, liabilities, net worth, annual income sources, and contingent liabilities.
- **Section 2:** notes payable detail.
- **Section 3:** stocks and bonds detail.
- **Section 4:** real estate detail.
- **Sections 5–8:** descriptions of other property and assets, unpaid taxes, other liabilities, and life insurance held.
- **Signature and date.**

## Who has to fill one out

The statement is required from **every owner of 20% or more** of the business. It is often also required from **spouses and guarantors**.

Two practical points follow from that:

1. **The requirement attaches to people, not just to the business.** If three people each own 20% or more, all three are covered by it. [VERIFY: whether each person files a separate form or spouses may complete one together]
2. **Ask early who else needs one.** Whether a spouse or a guarantor must also submit a statement depends on your situation, so ask your lender at the start. Finding out late that another person needs to complete a form means starting that form from scratch at the last minute.

## Why SBA lenders require it

A business loan application tells the lender about the business. The personal financial statement tells the lender about the people behind it. It answers questions the business's own records cannot:

- **What do the owners personally own and owe?** The balance sheet in Section 1 lays this out line by line and reduces it to a single net worth figure.
- **How much could the owners reach quickly if they needed to?** Cash and savings are reported separately from assets such as real estate and personal property, which makes liquidity visible.
- **What obligations do the owners already carry?** Notes, installment loans with their monthly payments, mortgages, and unpaid taxes are all listed.
- **Is anything waiting in the wings?** The contingent liabilities part of Section 1 covers debts that are not owed today but could become the owner's responsibility.
- **Does the person stand behind the numbers?** The form ends with a signature and date.

In short, the statement lets a lender see the personal financial position of each major owner in a standard format, so that every applicant is described the same way.

## What lenders check

When a lender reads the statement, four things get checked.

### 1. Does it balance?

Section 1 is built on one equation:

**Net worth = total assets − total liabilities**

The first check is simple arithmetic. Do the asset lines add up to the total assets shown? Do the liability lines add up to the total liabilities shown? Does the subtraction produce the net worth you wrote down?

A statement that does not balance signals that the figures were not checked, and it raises doubt about every other number on the page. This is the easiest problem to prevent: add each column twice, and redo the subtraction.

### 2. Do the schedules match the summary?

Section 1 is a summary, and the later sections are the detail behind it. A reader will compare them:

| Section 1 line | Should agree with |
| --- | --- |
| Notes payable | Total current balances in Section 2 |
| Stocks and bonds | Total in Section 3 |
| Real estate (asset) | Total market value in Section 4 |
| Mortgages on real estate (liability) | Total mortgage balances in Section 4 |
| Other personal property; other assets | Descriptions in Section 5 |
| Unpaid taxes | Description in Section 6 |
| Other liabilities | Description in Section 7 |
| Life insurance cash surrender value; life insurance loans | Description in Section 8 |

When a Section 1 line shows an amount and its schedule is empty — or the schedule lists items that never reach Section 1 — the statement is inconsistent with itself. Check every row of the table above before you submit.

### 3. Liquidity

Net worth alone does not show how much flexibility you have. Two people can report the same net worth while one holds mostly cash and savings and the other holds mostly real estate and personal property.

Lenders therefore look at the liquid lines — cash and savings in particular — next to the obligations that require regular payments, such as the installment accounts (which ask for monthly payments) and the notes in Section 2 (which ask for the payment amount and frequency).

You cannot change your liquidity by the way you fill in the form, and you should not try. What you can do is report it clearly: put each asset on the correct line, so that liquid assets are not buried under "other assets," and make sure every payment amount is filled in.

### 4. Signature and a current date

A statement that is unsigned is incomplete. A statement that is signed but undated, or dated long ago, creates a different problem: the reader cannot tell whether the figures still describe your finances.

Two dates matter on Form 413:

- The **statement date** in the header, which is the "as of" date for the numbers.
- The **date next to your signature** at the end.

Both should be current when you submit. How recent is recent enough is a question for your lender. [VERIFY: any specific maximum age for the statement date]

## How to prepare your statement

A clean statement is mostly a matter of order and patience.

1. **Choose the statement date.** Every figure should be as of that one date.
2. **Collect records.** Bank, retirement, and brokerage statements; loan and mortgage statements; real estate purchase records; life insurance statements; and records of any unpaid taxes.
3. **Fill in the detail sections.** Notes payable (holder, original balance, current balance, payment, frequency, collateral); stocks and bonds; real estate (type, address, purchase date, cost, market value, mortgage holder, balance, payment, status); and the descriptions in Sections 5 through 8.
4. **Carry totals to Section 1.** Copy each schedule total to its summary line.
5. **Complete the rest of Section 1.** Cash, savings, IRA / retirement, accounts and notes receivable, life insurance cash surrender value, automobiles, accounts payable, both installment lines with monthly payments, annual income sources, and contingent liabilities.
6. **Do the math.** Total the assets, total the liabilities, subtract.
7. **Complete the header.** Name and address, business name, statement date, loan purpose, business type, marital status, SSN.
8. **Sign and date.**

## Mistakes that slow an application down

- **Describing the business instead of yourself.** The form is personal. Business assets and business debts belong in the business's own financial statements.
- **Using the wrong life insurance number.** The asset line asks for cash surrender value, not the policy's face amount.
- **Leaving lines blank.** A blank can mean "zero" or "forgot." Ask your lender how they want lines that do not apply to be marked. [VERIFY: whether the form's instructions specify how to mark non-applicable lines]
- **Forgetting debts tied to assets.** A car listed under automobiles usually has a matching entry under the auto installment line if it is financed. A property in Section 4 with a mortgage needs the holder, balance, payment, and status filled in.
- **Mixing dates.** A bank balance from one month and a brokerage balance from another make the statement harder to verify.
- **Leaving out contingent liabilities.** If you have co-signed or guaranteed someone else's debt, it belongs in the contingent liabilities part of Section 1 even though you are not making the payments.
- **An owner left out.** The statement is required from every owner of 20% or more.

## Pre-submission checklist

Work through this list with the completed form in front of you.

### Header

- [ ] Applicant name and address are your personal details
- [ ] Business name is filled in
- [ ] Statement date is filled in and current
- [ ] Loan purpose is marked: 7(a) / 504 / surety, 8(a), WOSB, or disaster loan
- [ ] Business type is marked
- [ ] Marital status is marked
- [ ] Social Security number is entered and correct

### Section 1 — summary

- [ ] Every asset line is addressed: cash, savings, IRA / retirement, accounts and notes receivable, life insurance cash surrender value, stocks and bonds, real estate, automobiles, other personal property, other assets
- [ ] Every liability line is addressed: accounts payable, notes payable, installment account (auto), installment account (other), life insurance loans, mortgages, unpaid taxes, other liabilities
- [ ] Both installment lines show the balance **and** the monthly payment
- [ ] Asset lines add up to total assets
- [ ] Liability lines add up to total liabilities
- [ ] Net worth equals total assets minus total liabilities
- [ ] Annual income sources are completed with yearly amounts
- [ ] Contingent liabilities are completed

### Sections 2–4 — schedules

- [ ] Section 2: each note shows holder, original balance, current balance, payment, frequency, and collateral
- [ ] Section 2 current balances total the notes payable line in Section 1
- [ ] Section 3 total equals the stocks and bonds line in Section 1
- [ ] Section 4: each property shows type, address, purchase date, cost, market value, mortgage holder, mortgage balance, payment, and status
- [ ] Section 4 market values total the real estate line in Section 1
- [ ] Section 4 mortgage balances total the mortgages line in Section 1

### Sections 5–8 — descriptions

- [ ] Section 5 explains the other personal property and other assets amounts
- [ ] Section 6 explains the unpaid taxes amount
- [ ] Section 7 explains the other liabilities amount
- [ ] Section 8 describes life insurance held, consistent with the cash surrender value and life insurance loan lines

### Signature

- [ ] The form is signed
- [ ] The signature is dated, and the date is current
- [ ] No figures were changed after signing

### The whole package

- [ ] Every owner of 20% or more is covered by a completed statement
- [ ] You have asked your lender whether a spouse or any guarantor must also complete one
- [ ] You are using the current version of the form (05-24, expires 08/31/2027)
- [ ] You have kept a copy of what you submitted, along with the records behind each figure

## The bottom line

The personal financial statement is the lender's standard view of each major owner's personal finances. Lenders look first at whether it balances, whether the schedules match the summary, how liquid you are, and whether it carries a signature and a current date. Three of those four are entirely within your control on the day you fill out the form.

A complete, consistent statement does not guarantee approval, and no guide can promise one. What it does is make sure your application is judged on your actual finances rather than held up by a form that has to be sent back.
